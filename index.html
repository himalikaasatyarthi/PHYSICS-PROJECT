Where do we use these coordinates?

Cartesian · Motion in a lab

Track a moving object using its left/right, forward/back and up/down position. Useful for projectile motion and 3D mechanics.

🧲

Cylindrical · Around a wire

When a long straight wire creates a circularly symmetric magnetic field, distance from the wire and angle around it are natural measurements.

🪐

Spherical · Planets & fields

For a planet or an ideal point source, distance from the centre and direction are often more useful than three separate axes.

🌀

Polar · Motion on a plane

Describe a point on a flat surface using its distance from a centre and its direction. Useful in planar rotation and orbit diagrams.

05 / Learn the language

Four ways to describe a point

Think of each system as a different set of directions to the same destination.

Cartesian (x, y, z)

Move along three straight axes: sideways (x), forward/back (y), then up/down (z). Like finding a seat using row, column and floor.

x, y, z

Polar (r, θ)

On a flat plane, move a distance r away from the centre and turn by angle θ from the positive x direction. It is 2D.

x = r cos θ · y = r sin θ

Cylindrical (r, θ, z)

Polar coordinates plus a height. Imagine a point on a round water tank: distance from the middle, direction around it, and height.

x = r cos θ · y = r sin θ · z = z

Spherical (ρ, θ, φ)

Measure the straight-line distance from the centre, turn around the vertical axis, then measure down from the positive z-axis. Like locating a star.

x = ρ sin φ cos θ · y = ρ sin φ sin θ · z = ρ cos φ
